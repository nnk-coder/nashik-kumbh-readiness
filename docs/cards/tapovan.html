If the hour's arrivals all stood in the precinct at once the density would be 4.7/m² — Fruin band E. They physically cannot, and that is the point: the excess does not disappear, it backs up along the approach routes, which is where crowd collapse happens.

Losing one facility cluster

  • Toilets: 6 units in 1 cluster, the largest holding 100% of them. The queue already never clears, so a closure only deepens it.
  • Water points: 3 units in 1 cluster, the largest holding 100% of them. The queue already never clears, so a closure only deepens it.

Facilities within 120 m of each other are treated as one cluster: blocks that close together usually share a water tanker, a desludging route and a power feed, so they tend to fail together. A sanitation outage becomes a crowd problem when its demand walks off to find the next block.

  • Toilets, at what is mapped today: the queue never clears. 189,930 people an hour against capacity for 120; the backlog grows by 189,810 every hour.
  • Toilets, at the CPHEEO norm: about 0 minutes of queueing at 25,180 units.
  • Water points, at what is mapped today: the queue never clears. 303,888 people an hour against capacity for 180; the backlog grows by 303,708 every hour.

Queues are computed per block of 20 units, not across the whole site: people queue at the block in front of them. Pooling every unit into one queue would make the wait collapse to almost nothing, which is not what anyone has experienced at a mela. OpenStreetMap is not an inventory — a facility that exists but is unmapped counts as missing here, so the gap is an upper bound until the Authority supplies its own asset list.

  • FLAGIs any approach route dangerously narrow?15 ptsRaised when the narrowest link on an approach route is under 6 m wide. This is the mechanism that killed 39 people at Ramkund in 2003.
  • FLAGAre there enough toilets?10 ptsRaised when more than 20% of the seats required by CPHEEO's community toilet norm are missing from the map.
