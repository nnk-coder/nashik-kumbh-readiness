Four bathing sites. One pre-dawn hour. How many people can actually stand there?

The Nashik–Trimbakeshwar Simhastha runs from 31 October 2026 to 24 July 2028. The risk concentrates on four Amrit Snan mornings in August and September 2027, when the state expects around 12 crore visitors in total — roughly four times the 2015 event.

This measures the open ground at each bathing site, works out what it can process in the busiest hour, says where the people it cannot process are supposed to go, checks the toilets and drinking water against national norms, and then asks what one closure would do to all of it.

Everything here comes from public map data and published standards. No account, no licence, no site visit.

The problem

This has already happened, twice, in living memory

Crowd collapse at a Kumbh is not a freak event. It has a mechanism, and the mechanism repeats: more people are fed towards a point than the ground at that point can clear, and then something closes.

Nashik now expects roughly four times the crowd it handled in 2015, on ghats inside a dense historic city rather than on open sandbanks, during the monsoon rather than the dry season.

Predictability

How much of it can you see coming?

The honest answer is: most of the arithmetic, none of the behaviour. 4 of 6 failure modes below are visible from data that already exists, years before the event. The rest are not, and no amount of mapping will make them so.

Failure modeForeseeable?How far aheadWhy
A route too narrow for the crowd sent down itYesYears aheadStreet widths and the open ground at each ghat are in open map data today.
One closure removing most of the exit capacityYesYears aheadLeave-one-out on the route network. No new data needed.
Overflow with nowhere to goYesYears aheadOpen space within walking distance is mapped; so is how much of the surplus it absorbs.
Toilet and water queues collapsingMostlyMonths aheadRequires the authority's own facility inventory to be exact; open data gives the order of magnitude.
Where a crowd will actually choose to goNo—Behavioural, and needs a dynamic simulation plus on-the-ground stewarding plans.
A barrier failing, or a decision taken at 1amNo—Operational. Mitigated by redundancy, which is exactly what the failure analysis measures.

That split is the argument for doing this early. The foreseeable half is cheap to check and expensive to discover late — widening an approach route is a civil works decision with a lead time of years, not a decision anyone can take on the morning of a Snan.

How it works

From a map anyone can download to a number you can argue with

Step 1 in detail: where the crowd number comes from

Every multiplier is a stated assumption living in one configuration file. The peak-day share follows Prayagraj 2025, where about a quarter of expected attendance came on a single morning; Nashik spreads across more Snan days, so 15% is used here. Disagree with any of them and the whole page recalculates.

Demand against what the ground can take

"Can process" is standing capacity multiplied by how many times the space turns over in an hour at a 20-minute dwell. Throughput, not instantaneous capacity, is what binds at a bathing ghat: people arrive, bathe and leave.

Single points of failure

How much rides on one route

At Prayagraj in 2025 the crush was made worse by exits and pontoon bridges being closed while people were already moving. The capacity existed on paper and was not available on the night. So each site is also tested by removing one thing at a time.

Share of a site's total exit capacity carried by its single busiest route. Above 40% there is no meaningful alternative: closing it is not a degradation, it is an incident. The same test runs over holding grounds and over toilet and water clusters.

What the risk score is made of

0–24 no change needed on this evidence 25–54 resolve before the Snan 55–100 several checks failed

Mean evidence resolved across sites: 100%. An unresolved check is reported as unresolved and scored as clear, so every score here is a lower bound.

What it is worth

The cheapest thing you can do before anyone travels

A site assessment the usual way

Weeks

  • Engineers and stewards walk each site
  • One site at a time, in sequence
  • Cost scales with every site added
  • Describes the site as it is today

This, first

Minutes

  • Runs from a desk, before anyone travels
  • Every site at once, re-runnable whenever plans change
  • Data costs nothing; the tenth site is free
  • Tests the plan against arithmetic, not impressions

It does not replace a site visit. It decides which sites are worth one, what to measure when you get there, and which questions to put to the people who already know the ground. On this run it puts a number on something no walk-round would surface: 1,185,728 people with nowhere to go in a single hour, and the specific routes, holding grounds and facility clusters whose loss would make that worse.

Six things this gets wrong — and why they are published

  1. OpenStreetMap is not an asset register. A toilet that exists but was never mapped counts as missing, so the sanitation gaps are an upper bound until the authority supplies its own inventory.
  2. Most road widths are inferred, not measured. Indian streets rarely carry a width tag, so widths come from road class. Each card says what share was inferred; this is the first thing a site visit replaces.
  3. Open ground is an upper bound. It is the precinct less buildings and water. Barriers, akhara enclosures, vehicle routes and stalls all take more away.
  4. The arrival assumptions are assumptions. Peak-day and peak-hour shares are reasoned from Prayagraj 2025, not observed at Nashik, and the site weights are a planning split rather than a forecast.
  5. Queueing assumes people spread evenly between blocks. They do not, so real waits are worse than the figures here.
  6. Nothing here models behaviour. Crowd collapse is a dynamic process; this is a static capacity check. It finds sites that cannot work arithmetically. A site that passes still needs a simulation.

And the standing rule: this is a screening tool, not a safety case. It is not a prediction of harm, and not a statement about any authority's preparations.
