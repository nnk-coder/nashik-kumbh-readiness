Four bathing sites. One pre-dawn hour. How many people can actually stand there?

The Nashik–Trimbakeshwar Simhastha runs from 31 October 2026 to 24 July 2028, and the risk concentrates on four Amrit Snan mornings in August and September 2027. The state expects around 12 crore visitors, roughly four times the 2015 event.

This measures the open ground at each bathing site from OpenStreetMap, works out what it can process in the busiest hour, and says where the people it cannot process are supposed to go. It then does the same for toilets and drinking water against CPHEEO's norms.

Two things make Nashik unlike Prayagraj, and both make the Prayagraj playbook a poor template. Nashik's ghats sit inside a dense historic city rather than on open sandbanks — in 2003, 39 people died at Ramkund when about 30,000 were held on a narrow approach street. And the Snans fall in the monsoon, not the dry season.

How the number is built

Twelve crore becomes thirty-six lakh in one hour

Every multiplier is a stated assumption, set in one config file and listed on each card. The peak-day share follows Prayagraj 2025, where roughly a quarter of expected attendance came on Mauni Amavasya; Nashik spreads across more Snan days, so 15% is used here.

Six things this gets wrong — and why they are published

  1. OpenStreetMap is not an asset register. A toilet that exists but was never mapped counts as missing. The sanitation gaps are an upper bound until the Authority supplies its own inventory.
  2. Most road widths are inferred, not measured. Indian streets rarely carry a width tag, so widths come from road class. Each card says what share was inferred; this is the first thing a site visit replaces.
  3. Open ground is an upper bound. It is the precinct less buildings and water. Barriers, akhara enclosures, vehicle routes and stalls all take more away.
  4. The arrival assumptions are assumptions. Peak-day and peak-hour shares are reasoned from Prayagraj 2025, not observed at Nashik. The site weights are a planning split, not a forecast.
  5. Queueing assumes people spread evenly between blocks. They do not, so real waits are worse than the figures here.
  6. Nothing here models behaviour. Crowd collapse is a dynamic process; this is a static capacity check. It finds sites that cannot work arithmetically. A site that passes still needs a simulation.

And the standing rule: this is a screening tool, not a safety case. It is not a prediction of harm and not a statement about any authority's preparations.
